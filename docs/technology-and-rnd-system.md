# Technology and R&D System

Status: normative pre-implementation design.
Version: 1.0.

## Purpose

Postcard City must represent technology as a capacity built over time, not as a universal score. A city can become a technology leader, remain specialised in a useful niche, or fall behind because it lacks money, skills, institutions, energy, infrastructure, or access to knowledge.

Research and development is an investment decision under uncertainty. Spending more does not guarantee a breakthrough, and a breakthrough does not guarantee local usefulness.

## Technology capacity

Technology capacity is composed of:

- education and technical skills;
- research institutions and laboratories;
- public administration capability;
- private-sector absorption capacity;
- digital and physical infrastructure;
- energy, water, and connectivity reliability;
- access to finance and procurement;
- openness to collaboration and knowledge transfer;
- equality of access and workforce inclusion;
- resilience, maintenance, and cybersecurity.

The player should see the components, not only one opaque technology number.

## R&D decisions

The player may invest in:

- basic research;
- applied research;
- public digital infrastructure;
- climate adaptation and water technology;
- health and care innovation;
- energy and storage;
- mobility and logistics;
- construction and building safety;
- tourism technology and cultural preservation;
- industrial diversification;
- cybersecurity and emergency communications;
- education, apprenticeships, and reskilling.

Every project has a portfolio profile: cost, duration, risk, dependencies, expected users, maintenance burden, spillovers, ethical concerns, and possible failure modes.

## Uncertainty and failure

Projects may:

- succeed and create local capability;
- succeed scientifically but have no local market;
- solve the wrong problem;
- arrive after the crisis has passed;
- be overtaken by a competing technology;
- become too expensive to maintain;
- create useful spillovers outside the original objective;
- fail technically or institutionally;
- be captured by a contractor or political network;
- increase inequality or dependence on a supplier.

Failure must be explainable. The game should distinguish bad luck, weak design, insufficient capability, corruption, poor procurement, and a mismatch between research and local needs.

## Leadership and lagging indicators

A city is not “advanced” because it owns a prestigious project. Leadership requires durable adoption, local skills, maintenance, productivity, resilience, and equitable benefits.

Indicators may include:

- patents and open knowledge;
- startup and supplier formation;
- research employment;
- productivity and diversification;
- public-service quality;
- energy and water efficiency;
- digital inclusion;
- technology export or dependence;
- maintenance backlog;
- talent retention and attraction;
- public trust in institutions.

The game must show lagging indicators and opportunity cost. Spending on a fashionable technology can crowd out housing, inspections, hospitals, or basic infrastructure.

## Technology trajectories

Projects belong to trajectories with prerequisites, alternatives, and path dependence. Examples include:

- resilient water city;
- safe and circular construction;
- regional health and care hub;
- clean energy and storage cluster;
- smart logistics and port modernization;
- industrial and agricultural technology;
- cultural preservation and low-impact tourism;
- public digital infrastructure.

A trajectory may be appropriate for one city and harmful or unaffordable for another. The explanation must identify context, assumptions, uncertainty, and affected groups.

## Complexity levels

| Level | Technology behavior |
|---|---|
| Relaxed | Small portfolio, visible outcomes, limited dependencies, generous feedback |
| Standard | Multiple projects, opportunity costs, adoption lag, and occasional failure |
| Advanced | Path dependence, supplier lock-in, unequal access, workforce constraints, and project portfolios |
| Expert | Research portfolios, correlated failures, geopolitical technology shocks, cyber risk, spillovers, and contested evidence |

## Quality gates

Before implementation, every technology project needs:

- a problem statement;
- a plausible mechanism of benefit;
- dependencies and prerequisites;
- implementation and maintenance costs;
- failure and misuse boundaries;
- distributional effects;
- measurable leading and lagging indicators;
- an explanation report;
- localization and accessibility treatment.
