# Crisis Catalog Coverage

This catalog expands the crisis design beyond spectacular disasters. Every crisis is a governance problem with prevention, preparedness, response, recovery, uncertainty, distributional effects, and a player-readable explanation.

## Natural and environmental

- heat wave and urban heat-island emergency;
- flood, coastal surge, storm, and prolonged rainfall;
- drought, water scarcity, and reservoir failure;
- wildfire, smoke episode, and protected-area fire;
- earthquake, landslide, sinkhole, and subsidence;
- extreme cold, snow, ice, and energy demand shock;
- sea-level rise, erosion, salinization, and chronic coastal damage;
- invasive species, crop failure, fishery collapse, and ecosystem loss;
- pollution episode, air-quality crisis, toxic spill, and contaminated water;
- waste-treatment failure and landfill emergency.

## Buildings and infrastructure

- unsafe building discovered after inspection;
- partial or total building collapse;
- bridge, tunnel, road, rail, port, or airport closure;
- dam, retaining wall, elevator, crane, or construction-site failure;
- electrical-grid, gas, heating, cooling, water, sewage, or telecommunications outage;
- cyberattack or ransomware affecting public services;
- failure of emergency communications or incompatible agency systems;
- shortage of construction materials and delayed reconstruction.

## Transport and industrial

- major road, rail, maritime, or aviation accident;
- hazardous-materials or chemical incident represented at a non-operational level;
- industrial fire, explosion, radiation alert, or refinery shutdown;
- port closure, logistics disruption, fuel shortage, or supply-chain shock;
- mass-casualty incident requiring coordination and hospital surge capacity.

## Health and care

- seasonal epidemic, pandemic, or hospital overload;
- high-level biological hazard or suspected deliberate contamination, without pathogen instructions;
- food-borne illness and drinking-water contamination;
- medicine shortage, care-home crisis, or healthcare workforce exodus;
- mental-health emergency and long-term trauma burden;
- heat-related mortality risk among vulnerable residents;
- disruption of disability, elder-care, childcare, or social-support networks.

## Safety, crime, and social cohesion

- terrorism or attempted terrorism treated as a public-governance event;
- organized crime expansion, corruption capture, or intimidation of institutions;
- drug-market shock, overdose wave, or violence spillover;
- hate-crime spike, communal tension, or targeted intimidation;
- kidnapping or disappearance crisis handled through institutions, not tactical play;
- protest escalation, riot, curfew decision, or civil-liberties dilemma;
- prison, police, emergency-service, or court-system crisis;
- misinformation, disinformation, panic, and loss of trust;
- strike, lockout, essential-service stoppage, or public-sector breakdown;
- mass displacement after insecurity or the loss of habitability.

## Geopolitical and migration

- war affecting the region without turning the game into combat;
- blockade, sanctions, border closure, or diplomatic crisis;
- sudden refugee or internally displaced population arrival;
- evacuation of residents, expatriates, or visitors;
- disrupted food, energy, medicine, or technology imports;
- regional conflict causing tourism collapse and fiscal stress;
- international aid, solidarity, or mutual-assistance opportunity;
- disputed territory or jurisdictional conflict affecting service delivery.

## Economic and institutional

- tourism collapse, over-tourism backlash, or destination reputation shock;
- banking, debt, liquidity, or municipal-finance crisis;
- inflation, rent shock, wage crisis, or mass unemployment;
- collapse of a dominant employer or industry;
- failed public-private partnership or contractor insolvency;
- procurement scandal, corruption investigation, or audit finding;
- election crisis, coalition collapse, or loss of governing majority;
- constitutional, legal, or judicial ruling that changes local powers;
- tax-base erosion, pension liability, or public-debt refinancing shock;
- investor withdrawal, ratings downgrade, or infrastructure financing failure;
- data breach, privacy scandal, or loss of public records.

## Housing and demographic

- mass displacement caused by unsafe buildings;
- sudden rent and mortgage shock;
- vacancy trap in a depopulating district;
- homelessness and shelter-capacity crisis;
- landlord insolvency or social-housing arrears wave;
- construction boom followed by oversupply and stranded assets;
- ageing population and care-demand surge;
- youth exodus, essential-worker shortage, or skills mismatch;
- return migration after external crisis;
- conflict between residents, visitors, students, and seasonal workers.

## Design rule

The game should not treat all crises as random catastrophes. Their probability and severity should depend on exposure, prevention, institutional capacity, infrastructure condition, inequality, climate, economic structure, and previous decisions. Some crises are sudden; others are slow emergencies that become visible only after repeated warnings are ignored.

Every implemented entry must specify its minimum complexity level, opt-out behavior, warning signals, affected groups, direct and prevented harm, response choices, recovery path, distributional consequences, and explanation text.
